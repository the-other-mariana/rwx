I've been reading recently Virginia Woolf's book Jacob's Room. It's a book I had started years ago. I knew from some research back then that it revolved around Jacob's death and that it had so much symbolism about his death. So I started it. As I read it, I couldn't get a grasp of anything, not even cues about his death. Couldn't get it. I forgot to continue it. Put it in my shelf for years.
Then, after a terrible and heartbreaking fight with probably my closest friend, I stumbled upon it. That night, I went to my room, devastated. I couldn't sleep. Not one minute. I remember it was the night the first summer storm hit. Of course it was. Since I couldn't sleep, I picked up a book from my shelf. I have tons of books there that I haven't read. The one I picked up: Jacob's Room. As I started it, I read in the first page a few remarks I had researched. All those notes reminded me of what I had investigated back then about the book. As I read those, I got a different grasp of it. It sounded like it was about absence. I started reading it. And like magic, I understood it this time. It's a book about absence. Jacob's absence. 

Many times, Woolf describes scenes, conversations, situations, only for the end to say something, very small sometimes, about Jacob. A detail, only related to the subject she first started with. Only as a consequence. I felt like that was what absence feels like: when you know someone, and this someone is suddenly out of your life - death, conflicts, breakup - the person doesnt disappear. Remains in your life, not voluntarily, but as a *consequence*. A consequence of your routine perhaps, a consequence because of your memories, because of objects left behind, smells, places. But remains. And I felt that Woolf addressed exactly this. By doing this small style-like references to Jacob, only at the end of two other characters' conversation, after describing a building, after describing a journey from a lady, did she mentioned, briefly, Jacob. After a description of how a man cuts grass and stumbles upon a house and that house has this or that style, Jacob's room is there. Or after a lady gets on a train, sees and thinks about this lad that is smoking, thinks of what he's reading, why he's reading it, what she will do after, does Woolf hits you with a final "and that lad was Jacob". 

All the time you're looking for this main character, and it never appears. And when he does, it's so brief. She creates a longing, a constant yearning in the reader for the main character. And he appears only as a consequence of other's actions, other's trips, other's scenery, other's conversations. That's absence. She explores many ways in which absence is felt. There's a couple of pages that she writes about how we are looking through a window towards a Cambridge room where Jacob and some friend are discussing. You can feel as if you're looking from a distance. She even says: "Jacob's words were inaudible. Maybe he didn't even say anything". I was shocked. Absence is also distance. It feels like we cant fully grasp Jacob. But we are trying, even guessing what he says. Trying to see, trying to see, trying to see. A longing. A desperation. An absence. That's what I have been going through. I only see him as a consequence. 

Another passage that left me shocked. Jacob and his friend's mother are talking, together with other women at a fancy party. Of course only the mom is talking. In fact, you doubt Jacob is even *there* because its been a while since he's addressed. Then, after a while of exchanges between two women, one of them asks something, seemingly to Jacob (no name is ever mentioned). He *never answers*. The other characters keep talking as if nothing happened. Other time, a woman makes a remark about him, and right when another woman is about to talk about him, someone else arrives and *changes the topic*. After some time, *we know he arrives because a woman* says: "Ah, Jacob!", but not because we knew what he was up to at all. Everything is described, everyone's thoughts are described except for Jacob's. *But we know he's there*. This paints an indescribable emptiness. You feel a hole. A hole where Jacob is. Avoided, blank, but present. This passage shapes what's missing. Woolf finishes it with a small bit describing how Jacob is saying goodbye three times. Odd. Why the goodbye repeats itself? My god. That's exactly how memory and absence works: a hole, a goodbye that we never forget. Unanswered questions, interruptions, doubt of presence. No descriptions. A farewell. Those are things Woolf uses to make us feel an absence. A missing part. Something got cut off.

You know what? there's also a feeling attached to *being* gone. One thing is to watch through a window, and try to see past the glass, past the shadows, past the gestures. Another thing is to be watched. Be gone. I wondered whether someone was watching me through the window, whether someone was there at all. Wondering where someone was. The absence sometimes has two sides. All I got was to wonder, to doubt. Then, the buzz. Trying to grasp a reaction from a buzz, from an opinion, from a quick description. To feel absence is to also be absent.
